Here's a guide you can hand straight to your friend.

**Why planets hold still and stars twinkle**

Stars are so far away that even in a big telescope they're points of light with no width at all. A single thin beam has to cross about 100 km of turbulent air to reach you, and every pocket of warm or cool air bends it a little differently. The beam wobbles, brightens, dims, and shifts colour many times a second, and your eye sees that as twinkling.

Planets are close enough to have a real disc. Jupiter is roughly 40 arcseconds across, which is tiny, but it's still a disc made of thousands of separate beams. Each one wobbles on its own, so the wobbles average out and the planet looks steady. That's also why planets are the best targets on a shaky night: they tolerate bad air far better than faint fuzzy things do.

**Your first night**

- Start before dark. Set the telescope up while you can still see, and align the finder scope on a distant chimney or streetlight so it actually points where the main tube points.
- Let it cool. A telescope brought out of a warm house needs 30 to 45 minutes to reach outside temperature, or the view will swim like a mirage.
- Use the lowest magnification eyepiece first (the one with the biggest number printed on it, like 25 mm). It gives the widest, brightest view and makes finding things easy. Only go higher once something is centred.
- Pick easy, bright targets: the Moon (near first quarter, when the terminator throws long shadows across the craters), Jupiter and its four moons, Saturn's rings. Save galaxies for a dark site and a later night.
- Give your eyes 20 minutes away from phone screens and white light. Red light keeps your night vision.
- Sit down if you can. A steady body makes a steady image, and you'll see twice as much detail.

**What they most need to hear**

The first view through a small telescope is almost always smaller and dimmer than the photos. That's not a bad telescope; it's a real one. Photos are hours of stacked exposure, and your eye is working with photons arriving right now, some of them after travelling for centuries. As the astronomer Carl Sagan put it, "Somewhere, something incredible is waiting to be known." Keep the telescope somewhere you can grab it in two minutes, go out often rather than long, and let the detail build up over nights. It does.
