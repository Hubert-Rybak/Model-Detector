A refrigerator doesn't make cold. It moves heat from the inside to the outside, over and over, using a trick with a special liquid.

Here's the trick. When a liquid evaporates (turns into gas), it soaks up heat from whatever is around it. You've felt this: when sweat evaporates off your skin, your skin gets cooler. A fridge uses a liquid that evaporates at a very low temperature, so it can soak up heat even from food that's already cold.

The journey of that liquid, in a loop:

1. **Inside the fridge**, the liquid flows through thin pipes behind the back wall. It evaporates there and pulls heat out of the air inside. That's why the back wall feels coldest.
2. **The compressor** (the thing that hums) squeezes the gas hard. Squeezing a gas makes it hot, much hotter than the kitchen.
3. **Outside the fridge**, the hot gas runs through the black coils on the back. Because it's hotter than the room, heat flows out into your kitchen, and the gas cools down enough to turn back into a liquid.
4. A tiny valve lets the liquid squirt back into the inside pipes, where the pressure drops, it evaporates again, and the loop starts over.

So the fridge is really a heat pump: it carries heat out of the box and dumps it behind itself. Put your hand near the back of a running fridge and you'll feel the warmth that used to be in your milk.

Two things that follow from this. First, a fridge can't cool a room by leaving the door open, because all the heat it pulls out of the front comes right back out of the back, plus the extra heat from the motor. Second, the fridge works hardest when you stuff it with warm leftovers, because there's more heat to carry out.
