A fridge doesn't actually *make* things cold. What it really does is **move heat out** of the box. Cold is just "not much heat," so if you keep carrying heat away from your food, the food gets colder.

To do this, the fridge uses a special fluid called a **refrigerant** that travels around and around in a loop of thin tubes:

1. **It soaks up heat inside.** Inside the fridge, the refrigerant is icy cold and turns from a liquid into a gas, a bit like water evaporating. Evaporating takes heat, so the refrigerant pulls warmth out of the air and food around it. (You've felt this when you climb out of a pool and shiver. The water evaporating off your skin is stealing your heat!)

2. **It gets squeezed.** The gas, now carrying all that heat, goes to the **compressor**, the motor that hums. It squeezes the gas tight, which makes it very hot, even hotter than your kitchen. (A bike pump gets warm the same way when you pump up a tire.)

3. **It dumps the heat outside.** The hot gas flows through coils on the back or bottom of the fridge. Heat always flows from hot things to cooler things, so it escapes into the room. That's why the back of a fridge feels warm. As the gas loses its heat, it turns back into a liquid.

4. **It cools way down and starts over.** The liquid squeezes through a tiny opening, which makes it icy cold again, and then it heads back inside for another round.

So the refrigerant is like a delivery truck: it picks up heat inside the fridge, drops it off in your kitchen, and goes back for more.

A few helpers make the job easier. The walls are stuffed with **insulation** (kind of like a puffy winter coat), and the door has a rubber seal, so heat has a hard time sneaking back in. And a **thermostat**, which is a little temperature sensor, switches the motor on when things warm up and off when it's cold enough. That's why you hear the fridge hum, stop, and hum again.

Why bother? The tiny germs that spoil food grow much more slowly when it's cold, so the fridge keeps your milk and leftovers fresh for longer.
