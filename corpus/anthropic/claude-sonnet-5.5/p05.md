Short version: for the right person, an EV in 2026 is a genuinely good deal, but in the wrong situation it can be a headache. Here are the main trade-offs. A lot of the specifics (prices, incentives, electricity costs) vary by country, state and even utility, so treat the numbers as rough.

## Pros

- **Cheaper to run.** Electricity usually costs less per mile than gasoline, especially if you charge at home overnight. Maintenance is lighter too: no oil changes, fewer moving parts, and regenerative braking means brakes last much longer.
- **Nice to drive.** Instant torque, a quiet cabin, and smooth acceleration, with a low center of gravity that makes them feel planted.
- **Home charging is convenient.** If you can plug in where you park, you start every day with a "full tank" and rarely need a station.
- **Range and charging have improved.** Many current models do 250-350+ miles (400-550+ km) per charge, faster-charging cars can add a lot of range in 20-30 minutes, and in North America many non-Tesla brands can now use Tesla's Supercharger network.
- **Lower lifetime emissions.** Building the battery is carbon-intensive, but in most regions an EV still comes out well ahead of a gas car over its life, and the gap grows as grids get cleaner. Zero tailpipe emissions also helps local air quality.
- **More choice and often better deals.** There are more models at more price points than ever, including some genuinely affordable ones, and automakers often lean on discounts and lease offers to compete.
- **Batteries are holding up.** Degradation is typically gradual (often around 1-2% a year), and most batteries come with warranties of roughly 8 years/100,000 miles.

## Cons

- **Upfront price.** The gap with comparable gas cars has narrowed but often remains. In the US the federal tax credit ended in September 2025, and incentives elsewhere change frequently, so check what's actually available where you live.
- **Charging depends on your housing.** With a driveway or garage it's easy. In an apartment, or if you park on the street, it can be a real hassle, and public fast charging can cost about as much per mile as gasoline and isn't always reliable. A home charger also typically costs $500-$2,000 to install, more if your electrical panel needs an upgrade.
- **Road trips and extreme conditions take planning.** Charging stops are longer than fuel stops, range falls in freezing weather and at high highway speeds, towing can cut it sharply, and rural areas may have sparse charging.
- **Depreciation and fast-moving tech.** EVs have tended to lose value faster than comparable gas cars, and the technology is still improving quickly, so what you buy may feel dated sooner. (The flip side: used EVs can be great value, and leasing sidesteps the resale risk.)
- **Insurance and repairs.** Premiums are often higher, and serious damage, especially to the battery, can be costly. Out-of-warranty battery replacement is rare but expensive, and fewer independent shops can work on EVs.
- **Savings aren't guaranteed.** Electricity prices have risen in many places, and some governments are adding EV-specific registration fees or road-use charges to make up for lost fuel-tax revenue.
- **Not zero-impact.** Battery materials come with real environmental and social costs from mining, and the climate benefit shrinks if your grid leans heavily on coal.

## Who it suits

**Strong fit:** you can charge at home or work, your driving is mostly commuting and local trips, you'll keep the car for several years, and local electricity prices and incentives are favorable.

**Maybe wait, or consider a hybrid or plug-in hybrid:** you can't reliably charge at home or work, you tow regularly or do long rural drives, you live somewhere very cold and drive a lot, or you need the lowest possible sticker price with no incentive support.

## Bottom line

The case is strongest if you can charge at home and your driving is fairly predictable, because the running-cost savings and driving experience are real. It's weaker if you'd depend on public charging, or your budget is tight and incentives are thin. Before deciding, estimate the total cost over 5-8 years using your local electricity and fuel prices, check current incentives, and test-drive a couple of models. Leasing is worth a look if you're nervous about resale value or how fast the tech is moving.
