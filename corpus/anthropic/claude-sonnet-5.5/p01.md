# Your First Night with a Telescope

Congratulations on the new telescope! Here's a quick guide to your first night out, starting with something you've probably already noticed when looking up.

## Why stars twinkle but planets don't

The twinkling isn't happening out in space. It's happening in the air above your head.

Starlight can travel undisturbed for years, even centuries, then hit our atmosphere, which is full of drifting pockets of warmer and cooler air. Each pocket bends the light a tiny bit (the same reason the air shimmers over a hot road). The pockets keep moving, so the light's path gets jostled many times a second and the star seems to flicker. Near the horizon, where the light crosses much more air, it can even flash different colors.

Stars are so distant that each one is just a point of light, even in a telescope: one thin beam on one path, so when that path is jostled, the whole star flickers. A planet is close enough to be a tiny disc (Jupiter looks hundreds of times wider than the widest-looking star in the night sky). To your naked eye it's still a dot, but the light from each part of that disc takes its own path through the air, and each path flickers out of step with the others. The flickers average out into a steady glow. Think of a planet as a crowd of tiny stars twinkling at different moments.

That's a rule of thumb rather than a law (planets low on the horizon shimmer too), but through your eyepiece the difference is obvious: stars stay pinpoints however much you magnify them, while planets open into little discs.

It also matters tonight. The same unsteady air blurs telescope views, and astronomers call that "seeing." When the stars are twinkling hard, expect planets to look soft and wobbly at high power, like something seen through moving water. That's the sky's fault, not your telescope's.

## Before you head out

- **Never point the telescope at or near the Sun.** Without a proper solar filter that fits over the *front* of the telescope, a glance can permanently damage your eyes in an instant. (If yours came with a little filter that screws onto an eyepiece, throw it away. Those can crack.)
- **Practice in daylight.** Take the dust cap off (everyone forgets once), learn how the mount and focuser move, and line up the finder with something distant, like a chimney at least 400 m (a quarter mile) away. Doing this in the dark is miserable.
- **Let it cool down.** Set the scope outside about 30 minutes before you start, so the air inside matches the air outside. Warm air in the tube makes the image shimmer.
- **Observe outdoors, never through a window.** Glass and escaping house heat ruin the view.
- **Dress warmer than seems necessary,** and get a planetarium app like Stellarium to see what's up tonight. Switch it to red night mode.

## Once it's dark

1. **Start with the eyepiece that has the biggest number**, say 25 mm. A bigger number means lower magnification, which gives a wider, brighter view that's far easier to aim. (Magnification is the telescope's focal length divided by the eyepiece's, so a 900 mm scope with a 25 mm eyepiece gives 36×.) Beginners grab the highest power first and then can't find anything.
2. **Aim at the Moon, a bright planet, or a bright star.** Center it in the finder, look through the eyepiece, and turn the focus knob slowly until it's crisp. Don't worry if the image is upside down or mirrored; that's normal for many telescopes.
3. **Let your eyes adapt.** For faint things, give them about 20 minutes in the dark. One look at a bright white screen undoes much of it.
4. **Step up in power only when the view is sharp.** Ignore "600× POWER!" on the box. A sensible ceiling is about 2× per millimeter of lens or mirror diameter (140× for a 70 mm scope), and on most nights the air allows less.
5. **Expect things to drift.** The Earth's spin makes the sky turn 15° every hour, so at high power a target slides out of view within a minute or two. Nudge the scope gently, and don't lean on it.

## What to look at

- **The Moon** is the best first target whenever it's up. Look along the line between light and dark, where low-angled sunlight makes craters and mountains stand out. A full Moon is bright but flat.
- **Jupiter or Saturn,** if your app says one is up. Jupiter shows two dark cloud stripes and up to four tiny moons lined up beside it. Saturn shows its rings at just 30–50×.
- **Star clusters and fainter things.** The Pleiades look best at low power. Nebulae and galaxies show up as faint gray smudges, not colorful photos; looking slightly to one side of them makes them appear brighter.

## The thing to remember

Your first night is for learning your gear, not for seeing the universe. It will probably feel clumsy. You'll lose your target, bump the tube, and find the planets smaller and paler than in the pictures. That's normal, and it doesn't mean you're doing anything wrong: those photos are hours of exposure and processing, while what you get is one live glance, with your own eye, of light that left Saturn more than an hour ago.

So don't wait for the perfect night. Even 20 minutes on an ordinary one counts, and every session makes the next one easier. The moment it clicks, such as your first steady look at Saturn's rings, is worth all the fumbling.

As astronomer and TV host Jack Horkheimer used to sign off his show: "Keep looking up!"
