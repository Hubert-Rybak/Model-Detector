# Your First Night with a Telescope

Congratulations. You now own a machine for catching light that left its source anywhere from 1.3 seconds ago (the Moon) to a couple of million years ago (the Andromeda Galaxy). Here's what I wish someone had told me at the start.

## Why planets sit still while stars twinkle

Look up tonight and you'll notice a few bright "stars" that don't twinkle. Those are planets, and the reason is a nice little piece of physics.

Starlight reaches you through roughly 100 km of air, and air is never still. Warm and cool pockets drift around, each bending light a tiny bit, like looking at a coin on the bottom of a swimming pool. A star is so far away that even in a telescope it's a single point of light, one pinprick. When the air nudges that pinprick around, it visibly flickers and even shifts colour. That's twinkling.

A planet is not a point. Jupiter, for example, is a tiny disk about 40 arcseconds across (roughly 1/45 the width of the full Moon). Think of that disk as thousands of pinpricks packed together. The air jostles each one independently, but at any instant the jitters average out, so the planet looks calm. A big flat stone in a stream versus a pebble.

So, a handy rule: **steady = planet, twinkling = star.** (Caveat: anything near the horizon twinkles, planets included, because its light has to cross far more air.)

The flip side matters to you: that same turbulence, which astronomers call "seeing," sets the limit on how sharp your view can be on a given night. On a bad night, more magnification just gives you a bigger blur.

## Your first night, step by step

**1. Set up in daylight.** Point the scope at a chimney or treetop a few hundred metres away, centre it in the eyepiece, then adjust the little finder scope until it shows the same thing. Skipping this is the number one reason first nights end in frustration. You simply can't find anything.

**2. Let the scope cool.** Put it outside 30 to 60 minutes before you start. A warm telescope leaks heat into the tube and the view wobbles like a mirage.

**3. Start with the Moon.** It's bright, impossible to lose, and genuinely spectacular. Look along the *terminator*, the line between the lit and dark sides, where long shadows make the craters pop. A half Moon beats a full one, which is flat and glaring.

**4. Low power first, always.** Use the eyepiece with the biggest number on it (say, 25 mm). That's the *lowest* magnification and the widest view. Find the object, centre it, then swap to a shorter eyepiece. A rough ceiling for useful magnification is about 2x your telescope's aperture in millimetres (around 200x for a 100 mm scope), and most nights the air won't support even half that. 100 to 150x is plenty for planets.

**5. Things drift.** The Earth rotates, so at high power your target slides out of view in under a minute. Nudging the scope along is normal, not a defect.

**6. Protect your night vision.** Your eyes take about 20 to 30 minutes to fully adapt to the dark, and one glance at a phone screen resets the clock. Use a red torch, or set your phone's screen to red in the accessibility settings.

**7. Easy targets, in order.** Moon, then the brightest planet up (Saturn's rings show at just 25x; Jupiter's four big moons at any power), then a bright double star like Albireo or Mizar, then the Pleiades at low power, then the Orion Nebula in winter or the Andromeda Galaxy in autumn if your sky is dark enough. A free app like Stellarium will tell you what's up tonight.

**8. Dress warmer than you think.** Standing still outdoors at night is a different kind of cold.

**9. Never point it at the Sun** without a proper solar filter made for the front of the telescope. This isn't a "be careful." It's permanent eye damage in a fraction of a second.

## What you most need to hear

**The view will be smaller and fainter than the pictures on the box.** Those are long-exposure photographs. Your eye works in real time. Galaxies will be grey smudges. Saturn will be tiny. But it will be *Saturn*, rings and all, hanging in black space, and the light hitting your eye left it over an hour ago. The first time it swims into focus you will probably say something out loud.

Give it time at the eyepiece. Your brain learns to see: spend ten minutes on Jupiter and you'll notice cloud belts that were invisible in the first ten seconds. For faint things, look slightly to one side ("averted vision"); the edge of your retina is more sensitive than the centre.

And don't buy anything else yet. Learn this scope. The best telescope is the one that actually gets carried outside, and short, frequent sessions beat rare heroic ones. Leslie Peltier, who found a dozen comets from his backyard with modest equipment, wrote the only prescription you need:

> "One gentle dose of starlight to be taken each clear night just before retiring."
