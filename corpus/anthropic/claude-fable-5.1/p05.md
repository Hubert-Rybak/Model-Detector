Short version: in 2026 an EV is a clearly good buy for some people and a mediocre one for others, and the dividing line is mostly *where you can charge*, not the cars themselves. Here's the honest case on both sides.

## Pros

**Running costs are usually much lower.** Charging at home overnight typically works out to a third to a half of what gasoline costs per mile, and maintenance is simpler: no oil changes, far fewer moving parts, and regenerative braking means brake pads last a very long time.

**The cars are better and cheaper than they were.** There are now credible options in nearly every segment, including a growing group of sub-$35k models (the new Nissan Leaf, the returning Chevy Bolt, cheaper Tesla trims). 300+ miles of range is common, and the better 800-volt platforms charge from 10 to 80% in roughly 18 to 25 minutes.

**Charging is less annoying than it was.** Most brands have adopted the Tesla/NACS plug, most non-Teslas can now use Superchargers, and the fast-charging network is bigger and more reliable than it was two or three years ago.

**Battery fears have largely not panned out.** Real-world fleet data shows average degradation of roughly 1 to 2% per year, 8-year/100,000-mile battery warranties are standard, and LFP packs tolerate daily charging to 100%.

**The driving experience.** Instant torque, quiet cabin, one-pedal driving, and leaving home every morning with a "full tank" if you have a driveway or garage.

**Used EVs are a genuine bargain.** Because EVs have depreciated steeply, two-to-four-year-old examples often sell at big discounts, and battery health is now easy to check before buying.

**Lower emissions.** Even counting battery manufacturing, lifecycle emissions are lower than a comparable gas car on almost any grid, and the gap widens as the grid gets cleaner.

## Cons

**Upfront price, and in the US the federal credit is gone.** The $7,500 new and $4,000 used federal tax credits ended for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025. Some automaker, state, and utility incentives remain, but the sticker gap against a comparable gas car is real again. Tariffs have also pushed up prices on some imported models.

**Depreciation.** EVs have been losing value faster than gas cars. That is great if you're buying used, painful if you buy new and plan to sell in three years. Leasing can hedge this.

**No home charging makes the math much worse.** Public fast charging often runs $0.40 to $0.60 per kWh, which can rival gasoline per mile, plus the time cost. If you live in an apartment without workplace charging, think hard.

**Road trips, cold, and towing.** Expect 20 to 40 minutes per charging stop, coverage gaps in rural areas, 20 to 30% range loss in freezing weather, and towing can cut range roughly in half.

**Insurance, repairs, and tires.** Premiums tend to be higher, collision repairs can be pricier (and EVs are totaled more readily), the extra weight wears tires faster, and newer brands often have thin service networks.

**Policy and brand risk.** Incentives, tariffs, and emissions rules have shifted repeatedly; several EV startups have folded or struggled, and an orphaned EV is a software-dependent product. Many US states also now charge annual EV registration fees (typically $100 to $250) to replace lost gas taxes, and electricity rates have been rising in a number of regions.

**Fast-moving technology.** Range and charging speed keep improving (solid-state batteries are coming but not mainstream yet), which is part of why today's cars depreciate quickly.

**Home charger installation.** Budget $500 to $2,000 or more, and more again if your electrical panel needs an upgrade. Renters may not be able to install one at all.

## Who it makes sense for

- **Strong yes:** you have a driveway or garage, most of your days are under 150 miles, you'll keep the car five-plus years or lease it, and electricity is reasonably priced where you live. Also a strong yes if you're shopping used and verify battery health.
- **Maybe wait, or go hybrid/PHEV:** no home charging, frequent long trips through sparsely covered areas, regular towing, or very cold winters combined with thin charger coverage. A hybrid or plug-in hybrid captures a lot of the fuel savings without the charging dependence.

One caveat: this skews toward the US market. In Europe, China, and much of the rest of the world, cheaper models (especially from Chinese brands), denser charging, and different incentives shift the calculus noticeably toward "yes."
