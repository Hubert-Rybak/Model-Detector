It depends a lot on your situation, but the trade-offs are fairly consistent.

### Pros

- **Cheaper to run.** Electricity is usually cheaper per mile than gasoline, especially if you charge at home on a standard residential rate. Over several years, that adds up.
- **Less maintenance.** No oil changes and far fewer fluids to service. Regenerative braking also means brake pads often last much longer.
- **Convenient charging at home.** With a garage or driveway, you plug in overnight and start each day with a full battery and no trips to the gas station.
- **Driving experience.** Instant acceleration, quiet operation, and smooth power delivery. Many cars also improve through software updates.
- **Lower emissions.** No tailpipe emissions, and lifecycle emissions are generally lower than for a comparable gas car, though the gap depends on how clean your local electricity is.
- **Prices are coming down.** Battery costs have fallen, there are more models at more price points, and used EVs cost far less than new ones.

### Cons

- **Higher upfront price.** New EVs often still cost more than comparable gas cars, though the gap has narrowed. If you're in the US, the federal tax credit ended for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025. State, utility, and local incentives may still apply, but they vary widely.
- **Charging access matters a lot.** If you rent, park on the street, or live in an apartment, charging can be inconvenient. Public chargers can be slow, expensive, broken, or occupied.
- **Real-world range is lower than the sticker.** Highway speeds, cold weather (which can cut range by 20% or more, especially with cabin heat), towing, and hills all reduce it.
- **Road trips take more planning.** Fast charging has improved, but a long trip still means regular stops of 20 to 40 minutes, and only if the chargers you need are working and free.
- **Battery uncertainty.** Batteries lose capacity slowly over time. Most come with warranties around 8 years or 100,000 miles, but replacement out of warranty can be costly, and judging the battery health of a used EV takes extra checking.
- **Depreciation.** Many EVs have lost value faster than comparable gas cars. That's good if you buy used and a real cost if you buy new and sell in a few years.
- **Hidden costs.** A home charger installation can run from a few hundred dollars to a couple thousand, more if your electrical panel needs an upgrade. Insurance can be higher, tires wear faster because EVs are heavier, and some repairs require specialized shops.

### Rough gut check

An EV is probably a good fit if you can charge at home, most of your driving is local, you'll keep the car for four years or more, and your electricity rates are reasonable.

It's harder to justify if you can't charge at home, you often take long highway trips or tow, you live somewhere very cold, you might sell within a few years, or the price gap over a comparable gas car is large and no incentives apply.

To get a concrete answer, compare total cost of ownership for the models you're considering: purchase price minus incentives, plus energy costs for your annual mileage, insurance, and expected maintenance, over the years you'd keep the car. Prices, incentives, and charging networks change quickly, so check current figures for your area before deciding.
