Congratulations on the telescope. Here's what I'd want you to know before the first night, starting with why the sky looks the way it does.

## Why stars twinkle and planets don't

The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 4.2 light-years away, or roughly 40 trillion kilometers. From that distance, even the biggest stars are just points of light. Earth's air isn't still, though. Pockets of air at slightly different temperatures bend starlight by slightly different amounts, and as they drift past, a star's point jumps in brightness and position. That jumping is the twinkle.

Planets are much closer and have a real, if tiny, disk. Light from different parts of that disk gets bent a little differently, and those differences average out, so the planet looks steadier. Planets aren't immune, though. Crank up the magnification and even Jupiter will shimmer.

A telescope makes stars brighter but not bigger, since they still show up as points. Planets really do get bigger. Jupiter's disk spans about 40 arcseconds, and on a steady night you may be able to make out its cloud bands.

## Your first night

1. **Align the finder in daylight.** Center something far away, like a chimney or a road sign, in the finder and then in the eyepiece. It's much easier than doing it in the dark. Never point the telescope at the Sun unless it has a proper solar filter over the front. Looking at the Sun through an unfiltered scope can cause permanent eye damage, and it won't hurt, so you won't get a warning. Never use a filter that screws into the eyepiece.

2. **Let it cool down.** Set it outside 30 to 60 minutes before you start. A telescope warmer than the outside air gives a wobbly, boiling image, especially a reflector (the open tube with a mirror at the bottom).

3. **Give your eyes 20 to 30 minutes.** That's roughly how long they need to adjust to the dark. Use a red flashlight, since white light wipes out that adjustment.

4. **Start with the Moon.** It's bright, easy to find, and full of detail. It's about 384,400 km away on average, which is close enough to show craters and mountain ranges clearly. A half Moon is ideal, because the shadows along the edge of the light make the features pop.

5. **Use your lowest-power eyepiece first.** Magnification is the telescope's focal length divided by the eyepiece's focal length. If your telescope has a 900 mm focal length, a 25 mm eyepiece gives you 36x. Low power gives a wide, easy view for finding things. Resist cranking it up. High power magnifies the air's shimmer along with the object, so a sharp image at moderate power beats a blurry one at high power.

6. **Pick a few easy targets.** Use a free app like Stellarium to see what's up. Saturn's rings show even in small telescopes, and Jupiter's four big moons sit strung out beside it like tiny stars. Save the faint stuff like galaxies and nebulae for a moonless night, since they need dark skies.

7. **Use averted vision for faint things.** Look a little to one side of a faint object. The edges of your retina are more sensitive in dim light, so the object will often pop into view.

8. **Keep it short and comfortable.** Sit in a chair, dress warmer than you think you need, and aim for about an hour. You'll get better at seeing each time you go out.

## One last thing

Your first night will probably feel a bit underwhelming. Things will look fainter and grayer than in the photos, because those photos stack long exposures and your eye can't. That's not a sign something's wrong with the telescope or with you. Seeing through one is a skill, and you build it over lots of short, chilly, slightly disappointing sessions.

So judge each night by whether you saw something you hadn't seen before, even if it was only the Moon. Carl Sagan wrote in *Cosmos*: "The Cosmos is all that is or ever was or ever will be." You don't need a perfect sky to start looking.
