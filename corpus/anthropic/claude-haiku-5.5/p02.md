Here's a surprise: a fridge doesn't really make cold. It's more like a **heat thief**. It grabs warmth from inside the fridge and dumps it into your kitchen. Once the warmth is gone, the inside stays chilly.

It does this with a special liquid called **refrigerant**. This liquid boils (turns into a gas) at a super low temperature, so it changes easily. Each time it changes between liquid and gas, it picks up heat in one spot and drops it off in another. Here's its trip around the fridge:

1. **Squeeze.** A motor-powered pump called the **compressor** squeezes the refrigerant gas. Squeezing makes things hot. You've felt this when you pump up a bike tire and the pump gets warm.

2. **Dump the heat.** The hot gas flows through thin pipes called **condenser coils**, usually on the back or underneath the fridge. The heat escapes into your kitchen, and the refrigerant cools down and turns back into a liquid. That's why the back of the fridge feels warm!

3. **Squeeze through a tiny gap.** The liquid pushes through a skinny opening. When it bursts into a wider pipe on the other side, it gets very cold very fast, a bit like the chilly puff from a spray can.

4. **Grab heat from inside.** The icy refrigerant flows through **evaporator coils** inside the fridge. As it turns back into a gas, it soaks up heat from the air and food around it, like a sponge soaking up spilled juice. Many fridges also have a little fan that blows the cold air around so every corner stays chilly.

Then the gas heads back to the compressor, and the whole loop starts over. The fridge does this again and again, all day and all night.

A **thermostat** (a temperature sensor) tells the motor when to switch on and off, so the inside stays around 4°C (about 40°F).

So why does cold keep food fresh? Germs and mold are tiny living things that love warm, cozy places. Cold makes them slow way down, like a bear sleeping through winter. Food lasts longer because the germs are moving very slowly. Cold slows them down but doesn't stop all of them, which is why leftovers still go bad eventually.

And here's a trick you already know: when sweat evaporates off your skin, it takes heat away with it, which is why you cool off. The fridge uses that same idea inside its coils.
