# First Light: A Guide to Your Telescope's First Night

Astronomers call the first time a new telescope looks at the sky its "first light." Here's how to make yours a good one.

## Why stars twinkle and planets don't

Stars are so far away that even through a telescope they're just points of light. (The nearest one after the Sun is more than 4 light-years away.) Planets are close enough to be tiny disks. They're too small for your naked eye to notice, but they're disks all the same.

On its way to you, all that light passes through the atmosphere, which is never still. Pockets of warmer and cooler air drift around overhead, and each one bends light slightly differently. It's the same effect that makes the air shimmer above a hot road.

All of a star's light comes from a single point, so it all squeezes through the same narrow column of air. When that air shifts, the whole star jumps, dims, brightens and sometimes flashes colors. That's twinkling.

A planet's light comes from countless points across its little disk, and each one takes a slightly different path through the air. They all get jostled, but never in the same way at the same moment, so the jitters average out and the planet shines steadily. Candles work the same way. One candle in a draft flickers, but fifty in the same draft give a steady glow, because their flickers don't line up.

This helps you in two ways:

- **Spotting planets.** A bright "star" that isn't twinkling is probably a planet. (Low on the horizon you're looking through much more air, so even planets can twinkle there.)
- **Judging the night.** Astronomers call the steadiness of the air "seeing." If even the stars high overhead are twinkling hard, the seeing is poor and planets will look blurry at high magnification. On those nights, keep the power low.

## Before dark

**The one rule that beats all the others: never point the telescope, or its little finder, at the Sun.** Not even for a second. It concentrates sunlight enough to damage your eyes permanently in an instant. Safe solar viewing needs a proper filter that covers the *front* of the telescope. (If yours came with a dark "SUN" filter that screws into the eyepiece, throw it out. Those can crack from the heat.)

Then, in daylight:

- **Set it up and learn the knobs.** Everything is harder in the dark.
- **Align the finder.** Put in your lowest-power eyepiece and center something far away in the main telescope, like a distant treetop or pole. Then adjust the finder until it points at exactly the same spot. This ten-minute job does more than anything else to prevent a frustrating first night.

## Your first night

1. **Set up outdoors about 30 minutes before you start, and never look through a window.** The telescope needs time to cool to the night air. Otherwise it makes its own heat shimmer inside the tube.
2. **Give your eyes 20–30 minutes to adjust to the dark.** One glance at a bright phone screen undoes a lot of that. Use a red flashlight (or red cellophane over a regular one) and keep screens dim.
3. **Start with your lowest magnification.** That's the eyepiece with the *biggest* number on it, like 25 mm. It gives the widest, brightest, sharpest view and makes finding things far easier. If the box bragged about huge magnification, ignore it, because the air almost never allows it.
4. **Pick easy, bright targets.** A free app like Stellarium shows what's up and where. Turn on its red night mode. Good first stops:
   - **The Moon,** but not when it's full, because a full Moon looks flat and dazzling. When it's a crescent or half, look along the line between light and dark. The shadows there make craters and mountains pop.
   - **Saturn,** which is up in the evening sky right now (late 2026). Yes, you'll see the rings.
   - **Jupiter,** which rises a little earlier every night and will be an easy evening target early in the new year. Look for its cloud bands and the little moons lined up beside it.
   - **The Pleiades,** a glittering star cluster that looks best at low power.
5. **Keep it short and comfortable.** An hour and three objects makes a great first night. Dress warmer than you think you need to, since you'll be standing still, and bring a chair.

## Things that seem wrong but aren't

- **The view is upside down or mirror-flipped.** That's normal for astronomical telescopes.
- **Everything drifts out of view.** That's the Earth turning. At high power a planet can cross the whole view in a minute or two, so nudge the scope to follow it.
- **Stars look like blobs or donuts.** You're out of focus. Stars stay points no matter how much you magnify them, so focus until they're as small and sharp as possible.
- **Galaxies are faint gray smudges.** They really are. The colorful photos are long exposures. Try looking slightly to the side of a faint object, because the edge of your vision is more sensitive to dim light.
- **Planets shimmer and boil.** That's the same air that makes stars twinkle. Keep watching and you'll catch moments when it steadies and the detail snaps into view.

## The thing you most need to hear

Your first night will probably be a little frustrating. Everyone's is. Things will slide out of view, the focus will fight you, and the famous Andromeda Galaxy will look like a smudge on your glasses. None of that means you bought the wrong telescope.

Here's the secret: seeing through a telescope is a skill, and it gets better with practice. Experienced observers don't have better eyes. They've just learned to keep looking. Stay on Saturn for five minutes instead of five seconds and you'll start seeing things that weren't there at first glance. As Yogi Berra put it, "You can observe a lot by watching."

It won't look like the photos online. It'll be smaller, fainter and quieter, and it will be *real*. The light landing in your eye from Saturn left it over an hour ago and crossed more than a billion kilometers of space to get there. No photo can give you that.

Plenty of telescopes end up in closets after one disappointing night. Give yours three clear nights before you judge it, or yourself.
