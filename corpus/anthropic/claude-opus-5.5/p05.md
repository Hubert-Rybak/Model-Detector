It depends a lot on your situation, especially whether you can charge at home. Here's how the two sides compare.

## Pros

- **Cheaper to run if you charge at home.** Home charging often costs about half as much per mile as gas, and less on off-peak rates. The exact savings depend on your local electricity and fuel prices. Maintenance is lighter too: there are no oil changes and fewer moving parts, and regenerative braking makes brake pads last much longer.
- **Convenient and pleasant to drive.** With a home charger you start every day with a "full tank" and rarely need a charging station. The driving itself is a highlight: instant torque, a quiet cabin and smooth one-pedal driving.
- **More choice at lower prices.** Battery costs keep falling, and cheaper models are finally here. In the US, the revived Chevy Bolt and the new Nissan Leaf start around $30k, and Europe has several options around €25k. Used EVs can be excellent value, since the first owner has already taken most of the depreciation.
- **Easier charging than a few years ago.** In North America, most brands can now use Tesla's Supercharger network, and newer models increasingly come with the Tesla-style (NACS) port built in. Fast-charging networks keep growing almost everywhere.
- **Batteries are holding up.** Real-world data shows most battery packs lose capacity slowly, and battery warranties of 8 years or more are the norm. The fear of an expensive battery replacement after a few years has mostly not come true.
- **Lower lifetime emissions.** Even counting battery manufacturing, an EV usually produces much less CO₂ over its life than a comparable gas car, and it has no tailpipe emissions. The advantage is smaller where the grid relies heavily on coal, and mining for battery materials has real environmental and human costs.

## Cons

- **Higher upfront cost and fewer incentives.** EVs often still cost more than comparable gas cars. In the US, the $7,500 federal tax credit ended on September 30, 2025, which removed a big discount on both purchases and leases. Some states, utilities and other countries still offer incentives, so check what's available where you live.
- **Much harder without home charging.** If you live in an apartment or park on the street, you'll depend on public fast chargers. These can cost as much per mile as gas or more, and outside the better networks they're still unreliable. For many people, this is the dealbreaker.
- **Road trips, cold weather and towing.** A fast-charging stop usually takes 20–40 minutes, compared with five minutes at the pump, and rural trips need more planning. Range can drop 20–30% or more in freezing weather (a heat pump helps), and towing can cut it roughly in half.
- **Faster depreciation.** EVs have generally lost value faster than gas cars, because of price cuts on new models and fast-improving technology. That's good for used buyers, but costly if you buy new and sell within a few years.
- **Insurance and repairs.** Insurance often costs more, and collision repairs can be expensive and slow. Fewer independent shops work on EVs, and the extra weight wears out tires faster.
- **Policy and market uncertainty.** Governments are adding EV-specific road fees to replace lost fuel-tax revenue. Most US states now charge an extra yearly registration fee for EVs, and the UK plans a per-mile charge from 2028. Several automakers have also scaled back their EV plans. Startups are a particular risk: when Fisker went bankrupt, owners had trouble getting parts and service.

## Bottom line

An EV is probably a smart buy if you:
- can charge at home or at work,
- mostly drive predictable daily distances, and
- plan to keep the car five years or more, or buy used so the first owner takes the depreciation.

Think harder, or look at a hybrid or plug-in hybrid, if you:
- would rely entirely on public charging,
- often tow or drive long distances through areas with few chargers or harsh winters, or
- need the lowest possible purchase price or expect to sell within a couple of years.
